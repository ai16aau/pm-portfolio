What Can I Watch Now

The four personas

Persona 1 is the person I had in mind before I built anything, and I used it to work out what the product needed to do. Personas 2 to 4 come from the three user sessions, and they're who this release was built for. Most of the changes came from the differences between them.

Persona 1

Film-Night Farah

The invested viewer. Written before the build, as the person the original product was designed for.

  • Happy to spend real time getting a better recommendation. They'd sit down with a laptop or in front of the TV to do it, rather than check their phone in a queue.
  • Willing to answer a detailed question about every title picked, across a long list, because richer answers should mean a sharper result.
  • Already sold on the premise that a thumbs up is not enough, so needs no persuading on the landing page.
  • Watches mostly mainstream US and UK film and television, so an overwhelmingly US and UK catalogue fits.
  • Comes once, gets a great list, and has no need to come back.

Every one of those assumptions turned out to describe a minority of actual traffic. The next three personas are based on the people who actually used it.

Persona 2

K-Drama Kate

The viewer whose taste runs against the default. From User A: a parent of two, a heavy watcher of Korean drama and international film. She completed the journey and liked her recommendations.

What she said:

  • She could not select any Korean shows or films from the list of titles.
  • The mood step offered no international options, so her choices felt limited to Western and Hollywood productions.
  • She was happy with the recommendations but felt they could be more diverse in international reach.
  • Having picked her titles, she assumed that was the end, and was confused to then be asked why she liked each one.
  • Because she had picked a lot of titles, being met with that many options to work through felt daunting.
  • Told afterwards that the step was optional and skippable, she said she had not noticed.
Persona 3

First-Timer Theo

The first-time visitor. From User B: a 29-year-old privacy lawyer at a payments company. He did not complete the journey.

What he said:

  • He liked the look of the app.
  • He clicked lots of titles he liked, and thought that was the end of the journey and that it would then give him recommendations.
  • He then saw he had to say why he liked each one, and gave up because the idea of doing that was daunting.
  • Told afterwards it was optional, he said it had not been obvious.
Persona 4

Returning Rachel

The returner. From User C.

  • Asked whether preferences could be saved, so that coming back later in a completely different mood would not mean doing the whole journey again.

Persona photographs are free stock imagery, not the research participants; credits are at the foot of the page. Persona 1's detail beyond the design's own assumptions is inferred from what the original product was built to do.

What changed in the product

Three sources of evidence

I checked the problem three ways: moderated sessions with users, the journey funnel from the analytics, and a check of how the product itself behaved. They didn't all agree, and the places where they disagreed turned out to be the most useful.

Source 1

Moderated user sessions

Three users, in person: personas 2 to 4 above.

Source 2

The journey funnel

Cookieless analytics covering the product's full life to the pre-release read: 107 visits.

Source 3

The product's own behaviour

A check of the build and the catalogue against what the users described.

Also from that read
  • 63% of visits were on phones (46% phones, 17% tablets and large phones).
  • 64% United Kingdom, 27% United States.
  • No copy or email event had ever fired, so none of the four finishers took their list away.
  • Tagged inbound: from/linkedin fired 6 times, from/substack never.
Where the funnel disagreed with the interviews

The reasons screen lost one person in six, which is User B and no more. The larger leak was earlier: 23 of 34 people landed and never started at all. Neither interviewee mentioned it, and neither could have, because both were sitting with me and had already agreed to try the tool. Qualitative research is structurally blind to the people who leave before they become users.

Source 3: what the product check found

Rather than act on the users' explanation of what had gone wrong, I had the product checked against what they described. Three things came out of it:

1

The skip control was bound to a step that had been removed from the flow, so it never rendered anywhere in the product.

2

The only "this step is optional" wording sat in a bar that is hidden below 560 pixels of screen width, which is 63% of traffic. So on a phone there was no skip control and no optional text. Neither user failed to notice anything; there was nothing to notice.

3

The scoring already pooled every per-title answer into one global tally and never read which title an answer came from. Only the "similar to [title]" sentence in the results used it. So the screen people were abandoning was asking for work the model then discarded.

Why the check mattered

Taking the users' diagnosis at face value would have led to making the skip button more prominent, which would have achieved nothing, because there was no skip button.

Before and after the release

I checked each of these in the live build. They describe what the product now does. Whether it changes what people do is for the next funnel read.

A six-title session produces a 26-character code. The mood page runs about 1,200 pixels on a 390-pixel-wide phone, down from a page that also carried a year slider, a histogram, a region chip row and eight viewing-preference options.

How I prioritised

These scores were calculated to formalise the order the work actually ran in, using measured reach and the strength of evidence behind each item. They are a record of the reasoning, not a spreadsheet that existed on the day.

Reach is first visits per month at current volume, roughly 85 (34 over 12 days), reduced for items that only affect people who get further into the journey. Effort is in build days.

Why item 8 shipped on the lowest score

It came from one user, so confidence is low, and it only reaches people who finish. It shipped because it was cheap, because it was the only retention mechanism the product had, and because the design turned out to remove a compliance surface rather than add one.

Why item 9 was turned down

It was requested, and turned down. Nothing in the catalogue records who is in anything, so a question about actors would collect an answer with nothing to match against and would not change a single recommendation. Making it real means pulling billed cast for all 479 titles and building a matching rule.

How the release ran

I did the research first. The changes then went through five rounds of review, and I released them all together at the end of the same day.

  1. Round 1: the main fixes

    I replaced the per-title questions with one card and made the skip actually work. I added country and language to all 479 titles, then used them for a region filter, a new default order, region options on the mood step and a 60% cap on any one region in a list. I also changed the landing page to lead with the product, made copying the list the main action and added new analytics events.

  2. Round 2: one pattern for every filter

    I turned the chip rows, year slider and histogram into one dropdown pattern, with everything selected by default. The search bar got its own panel, because it wasn't obvious that people could search. I also added the returning-user code and cut the mood page down to about 1,200 pixels on a phone.

  3. Round 3: reordering the journey

    I moved the question about what you like to the start, before you pick examples. I cut the categories from ten to eight after a comprehension test, stopped offering the mood step as skippable, and made a restored code editable.

  4. Round 4: showing where you are

    Every screen now opens with a large step indicator and a line saying what comes next, because both users had thought they were finished when they weren't. I also moved the code to the top of the results.

  5. Round 5: final wording and release

    I finished the category wording and released everything. Then I ran a full journey on the live site to check it, with no errors.

Trade-offs

Keeping the mood step compulsory

I made the first step optional but kept the mood step. Mood is what makes the tool different, so I didn't want to offer it as something to skip.

Cutting categories I could not explain

I removed two categories and renamed one, because if I couldn't explain a category, a user wouldn't understand it either.

Asking once without losing signal

A preference you state now carries a weight of 3.0, against 0.3 for one inherited from a title you picked. That way, asking once didn't weaken the signal.

Labelling the stretch picks

Recommendations from outside the viewer's usual regions sit in their own block with a label. A recommendation someone did not ask for is only worth making if they are told that is what it is.

The Korean titles were there all along

User A said the product had no Korean content. It had six Korean titles. She was right about her experience and wrong about the cause:

  • Only 4 of the first 72 titles shown were non-Anglophone, and only 1 of the first 24.
  • The default order ran a hand-written popularity list of 148 titles, of which 9 were non-Anglophone, then sorted everything else by year descending, which sank older international cinema.
  • There was no language, country or region filter anywhere in the product.

The catalogue in numbers

I then fetched country and original language for all 479 titles from the TMDB API. The catalogue is 307 US and 82 UK, so 81% US or UK, with 52 titles in a language other than English and six in Korean: Parasite, Squid Game, Oldboy, The Handmaiden, Burning and Train to Busan.

What this meant

Reach was fixable immediately: surfacing and filtering what already exists is a code change. Depth is a separate, editorial job.

Choice paralysis is a data problem

The premise: choice paralysis on streaming services is a data problem, not a content problem. A thumbs up records that you liked something, never what you liked about it, so the signal cannot explain itself and cannot be reused.

What Can I Watch Now asks the question the thumb never does. It is built on a rating framework I designed: originally 11 categories of reason, 61 specific reasons, and 9 moods, applied by hand to a catalogue of 479 titles, 229 films and 250 series.

The three-stage scoring model

Stage 1: descriptive
0.3

What the title is

A layer carrying each title's own attributes, weighted at 0.3.

Stage 2: diagnostic

Why you liked things

A layer weighting the reasons a viewer states.

Stage 3: predictive
60 / 25 / 15

What you might like

Taste-profile similarity, co-occurrence across the catalogue, and genre affinity, blended in a 60/25/15 split.

Mood is applied as a separate re-ranking pass on top of the three stages.

Three balance rules, added after testing

Rule 1

Popularity damping of up to 35%.

Rule 2

One title per franchise.

Rule 3

A film-to-series mix matched to the viewer's own picks.

How it was built

I designed the framework, the taxonomy, the scoring model, the data and the acceptance criteria, and directed the build with an AI coding assistant, verifying behaviour before release. The code was written by the AI under my direction.

No server, no account, no personal data collected. All scoring runs in the browser. The site is hosted as a static site on Cloudflare Pages, with cookieless analytics.

Constraints

No server, no accounts, no personal data

All scoring runs in the browser on a static site. Saving preferences had to work without storing anything, which is why the code holds the preferences themselves.

One person

I did the research, design, data and requirements myself, with three research participants.

A small base

34 first visits and three interviews. That's enough to decide what to work on, but too few to measure the result.

A fixed catalogue

479 titles classified by hand, 81% US or UK. Surfacing what exists was a code change; adding depth is an editorial job.

No cast data

Nothing in the catalogue records who is in anything, which ruled out matching on actors this release.

Mostly phones

63% of visits were on phones, where the only "optional" wording had been hidden below 560 pixels of width.

A metric I discarded

Cloudflare reported 3,787 page views and 864 daily unique addresses over five days, against the funnel's 107 visits.

The country breakdown gave it away: single days with 277 requests from Brazil, 277 from India, 263 from Indonesia and 261 from the Netherlands. That is crawler traffic on a newly published domain.

I used the smaller, real number as the denominator and kept Cloudflare only as confirmation the site was being indexed.

The five requirement sets

These are the requirements for the release, in five sets. Each one covers the user problem, the persona it serves, the pain points, the requirements, what's out of scope and how it'll be measured.

1

The optional step

Serves persona 3, persona 2 secondarily
User problem

User B picked lots of titles, thought that was the end, then met a screen asking why he liked each one and gave up. User A, who had picked a lot of titles, found that many options daunting, and never noticed the step was optional.

Pain points
  • A per-title question repeated across every pick.
  • On phones there was no skip control and no optional wording at all.
  • The model pooled every per-title answer into one global tally, so the repetition was work it then discarded.
Requirements
  • One card asking what the user values, no per-title repetition.
  • Eight categories, one tap each, each with a plain explanation visible at phone width.
  • A skip control at equal visual weight to the primary action, plus an inline skip in the page body and a highlighted optional statement under the heading.
  • No loss of scoring signal: a single stated preference carries the weight repetition used to.
Out of scopeSampling a subset of titles to ask about. Rejected because it is still a per-title task and the basis of the selection cannot be explained to the user.
Metrics
  • Value step completed or skipped; skips by step.
  • Completion from first visit, 12% baseline, 25% target.
2

Regional and language discovery

Serves persona 2
User problem

User A could not select any Korean shows or films from the list of titles, and the mood step offered no international options, so her choices felt limited to Western and Hollywood productions.

Pain points
  • Only 1 of the first 24 titles shown was non-Anglophone.
  • No language, country or region filter anywhere in the product.
  • Six Korean titles existed but sat as deep as 430th in the default order.
Requirements
  • Country and original language on all 479 titles.
  • A region filter in the picker, and region plus language on the mood step.
  • Default ordering that surfaces non-US/UK titles early, drawing subtitled titles forward first.
  • A cap on how much of a result list comes from one region.
  • A separate labelled block of recommendations from a region none of the user's picks came from.
Out of scopeExpanding the catalogue.
Metrics
  • Region and language filter use.
  • Non-US/UK titles in the first 24 shown: 1 before, 6 in the shipped build.
3

Knowing where you are in the journey

Serves persona 3, and everyone who left the landing page
User problem

23 of 34 people landed and never started at all, and User B taps Continue expecting a list because nothing told him what came next.

Pain points
  • Progress signalling was a 4px bar and small grey text, hidden on phones.
  • The landing page led with explanation rather than the product.
Requirements
  • A prominent step indicator on every screen with number, step name, segmented progress and a line naming what comes next.
  • A compact position marker in the sticky header.
  • A landing page that leads with the action, with the explanation collapsed.
  • Steps reordered so that stating what you like precedes giving examples of it.
Out of scopeMoving the title picker itself onto the landing page, deferred so the contained version can be measured first.
Metrics
  • Landing to first step, 32% baseline, 50% target.
  • Journey step reached, renamed to match the new step order.
4

Returning without repeating

Serves persona 4
User problem

User C asked whether preferences could be saved, so that coming back later in a completely different mood would not mean doing the whole journey again.

Pain points
  • A return visit meant starting again from nothing.
Why this matters

Because nothing is stored, there is no consent banner to maintain and nothing to delete on request. Working within the privacy constraint made this a better feature.

Requirements
  • A code generated at the end holding the user's titles, categories, moods, date range, region, language and viewing preferences.
  • The code encodes the preferences themselves, so no server, no account and no stored record.
  • It appears on the results page and in the copied and emailed list, and can be pasted on the landing page to arrive at the mood step.
  • Restoring it offers a visible route back into the earlier answers, and changing anything regenerates the code.
Out of scopeAccounts, sign-in, any server-side storage.
Metrics
  • Sessions started from a code: 0 baseline, any measurable use as the target.
  • Code copied, code used, answers edited after a code restore.
5

Leaving with the list

Serves every finisher
User problem

No copy or email event had ever fired, so none of the four finishers took their list away.

Pain points
  • A finished list that stayed on the results page.
Requirements
  • Copy becomes the primary action, with the email handoff secondary.
  • A keep control on each recommendation so a title can be saved while reading.
  • Kept titles appear first in the copied and emailed text.
Out of scopeA mailing list, or any capture of an email address.
Metrics
  • Finishers taking the list away: 0% baseline, 40% target.
  • Titles kept.

Two scenarios, before and after

Scenario A: Persona 3, First-Timer Theo

Before

Opens a link from LinkedIn on his phone. Reads four paragraphs explaining how the tool works. Taps Start. Picks fifteen titles he likes. Taps Continue expecting a list. Gets a screen of fifteen cards, each asking why. Closes the tab.

After

Opens the same link. Sees one line on what the tool does and a button. Taps it. Lands on step 1 of 4, which says what he likes, with a highlighted line reading "This part is optional" and a skip beside it, and a line below saying what comes next. Picks three things that matter to him, or skips. Step 2 of 4 is the titles. Step 3 is the mood. Step 4 is the list.

Scenario B: Persona 2, K-Drama Kate

Before

Picks her titles. Scrolls a grid of American sitcoms looking for anything Korean. Finds Squid Game at position 11 and nothing else without paging five times. Gets recommendations that look like everyone else's.

After

Opens the picker, taps the "Made in" dropdown, taps Korea, sees six of six on one screen. On the mood step she sets a language preference. Her list comes back Korean, with a block underneath labelled "Outside what you usually pick" naming the region it came from.

Go-to-market

So far, the product has had one release, launched only through my own channels, with no paid acquisition.

  • Channels used so far: a LinkedIn post and a Substack technical write-up of how the model works.
  • Measured result: from/linkedin fired 6 times, from/substack never. All other traffic was direct, with 2 referrals from Google. Either the Substack link was untagged or the post sent nobody, and that is worth checking before drawing a conclusion.
  • Every visit is currently earned, so a visitor who leaves the landing page without starting is wasted reach. That is what makes activation the primary metric rather than traffic.

What I chose not to do

Catalogue depth

Still 81% US and UK with six Korean titles. The filter shows what exists. Adding depth means tagging more titles, which is editorial work.

Actor matching

Requested, and turned down for this release, because nothing in the catalogue records who is in anything.

Moving the picker onto the landing page

The stronger version of the landing page fix, deferred so the contained version can be measured first.

Instrumentation added

Titles picked, banded. Value step completed or skipped. Skips by step. Region and language filter use. Code copied, code used, answers edited after a code restore. Titles kept. Journey step reached, renamed to match the new step order, so the series before and after this release must not be read as one.

Go-to-market metrics

What I learned

  1. Interviews only reach people who've already agreed to take part. The biggest drop-off (23 of 34 people never starting) only showed up in the funnel.
  2. Check the product before acting on the diagnosis. Users said they had not noticed the skip. Taking that at face value would have meant making the skip button more prominent, and there was no skip button.
  3. The ranking mattered more than the catalogue. The Korean titles were there all along, just ranked too low for anyone to find them.
  4. If I can't explain an option, a user won't understand it. That test cut two categories.
  5. The privacy constraint made the feature better. Because nothing is stored, there is no consent banner and nothing to delete on request.
  6. Releasing everything at once makes results hard to attribute. The next read will show whether the journey improved, but not which change did it.

Caveats

Caveat 1

No outcome data exists. Everything shipped in one release, so the next funnel read will show whether the journey improved but not which change did it.

Caveat 2

The base is small. 34 first visits and three interviews. This is directional evidence, not measurement. No target should be read before roughly 150 first visits have accumulated.

Caveat 3

Figures marked as shipped and verified are verified behaviours of the build, not results.
